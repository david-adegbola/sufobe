# Video script draft: "How carbon from the air becomes a tree"

Target length: about 4 minutes (about 520 spoken words). Screen-record the prototype and narrate over it. Times are approximate; **[on screen]** lines say what to show.

---

**0:00 — The question** *(Predict screen)*
[on screen: step 1, van Helmont text, the four answer buttons]

> Almost 400 years ago, Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a small willow in a pot of soil and gave it nothing but water for five years. The tree gained about 74 kilograms. The soil lost about 57 grams. So where did the tree come from? Most people say the soil. Let's follow the carbon and find out.

**0:30 — Carbon dioxide in the air** *(Explore, paused, summer morning)*
[on screen: zoom on the O=C=O dots]

> The air around this birch contains carbon dioxide, about 420 molecules in every million. That's only 0.04 %, but it is where almost all of the tree's carbon comes from.

**0:50 — The way in: stomata** *(Play at "Hours" speed, dots flying into leaves)*

> CO₂ enters through stomata, tiny pores mostly on the underside of the leaves. Two guard cells open and close each pore. Every time a stoma opens to let CO₂ in, water vapour escapes. So the tree is always trading water for carbon.

[on screen: drag Water to the left; dots bounce off; HUD shows "Stomata: closed"]

> When the soil dries, the tree closes its stomata to save water. Now, even in full sun, CO₂ can't get in. That is why trees grow less in a drought year. It isn't because they've "run out of food".

**1:35 — Photosynthesis** *(leaves catching dots, sugar dots appearing)*

> Inside the leaf cells, chloroplasts use light energy to split water molecules. The oxygen from the water is released into the air. The energy captured is used by the enzyme Rubisco to fix CO₂ into sugar, in the Calvin cycle. In one line: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. The carbon and most of the mass in the sugar come from the CO₂. The hydrogen comes from water. The light is energy, not matter.

**2:15 — Respiration, day and night** *(switch to night; dots leave the tree)*

> But watch at night: CO₂ comes back out. Leaves, roots and the living layer of wood burn sugar for energy, all day and all night. In this model about half of what the tree captures goes straight back to the air.

**2:40 — From sugar to wood** *(orange sugar dots moving down the trunk; then "Fast-forward 10 years")*

> The remaining sugar travels as sucrose through the phloem to wherever the tree is growing. In the cambium, a thin layer just under the bark, it is built into cellulose and lignin: new wood. Some goes to roots. Some is stored for next spring, when a birch builds all its new leaves from sugar it saved the summer before. Each year's new wood makes a ring.

[on screen: the ring inset growing; point out a thin ring after a dry summer]

**3:20 — Weigh it** *(Weigh screen)*

> After ten years the dry tree has gone from about one kilogram to over 30. The soil lost only a few hundred grams of minerals. Dry wood is about half carbon. Counting every atom, about 93 % of this tree came from the air, 6 % from water, about 1 % from the soil, and nothing from sunlight.

**3:50 — Back to the air** *(What next? screen, chart)*

> The carbon doesn't stay forever. Fallen leaves are eaten by fungi and bacteria and breathed out within a few years. Wood made into a chair can hold its carbon for decades. Burning releases it all at once. Carbon always returns to the air. What changes is how fast.

**4:15 — Close** *(Ask again screen)*

> So: where does a tree come from? Mostly from thin air, built by sunlight. This is a simplified model of a single birch, but the mechanism is the real one: carbon in through stomata, fixed into sugar, partly breathed out, and the rest built into wood.
